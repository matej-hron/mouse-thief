1979 · Seattle, USA

The Prison That Turned Pink

In 1979 a researcher named Alexander Schauss persuaded the officers running a U.S. Navy correctional facility in Seattle to try an experiment: paint some of the holding cells pink and watch how the prisoners behaved.

The idea came from his own earlier tests. In one, young men stared at either a deep blue card or a bright pink one, and almost all of them tested weaker after looking at the pink. Schauss called the shade P-618 and claimed it lowered heart rate, pulse and breathing compared with other colours.

The colour was named Baker-Miller pink after the two officers, Gene Baker and Ron Miller. They reported that angry prisoners calmed down after about fifteen minutes in the pink cell. The news spread: psychiatric wards, police stations and holding cells were painted the same shade. By 2015 roughly one in five prisons and police stations in Switzerland had at least one pink cell, and there were pink cells in the US, the UK and Austria too.

The twist

When Schauss repeated his experiments a few years later, the calming effect did not show up, and a test in a real pink cell found no difference. In 2014 a team led by psychologist Oliver Genschow ran the first carefully controlled study in real detention cells and found that pink cells did not reduce aggression compared with grey ones. The researchers noted their study had only 59 people, so a very small effect could have been missed.

So the pink cells were real, but the evidence that they calm anyone down is weak. The early reports may have come from the surprise of a new colour, or from what the guards hoped to see.
